The Standard

Construct and revise an explanation based on evidence for how carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen from sugar molecules may combine with other elements to form amino acids and other large carbon-based molecules necessary for essential life processes.

You will use models and evidence to explain how the products of photosynthesis can help organisms build the molecules they need.

Step 1 of 11

1. From Sunlight to Sugar

Plants, algae, and some bacteria make sugar through photosynthesis. Plants take in water and carbon dioxide. They use light energy to help make sugar molecules. Oxygen is also released.

One common sugar made in photosynthesis is glucose. A glucose molecule contains carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen atoms. A plant can use glucose for energy. It can also use atoms from glucose to help build other molecules.

Plants can make sugar. This process is called photosynthesis. Plants take in water and carbon dioxide. They use sunlight to make sugar. They also release oxygen.

Glucose is one kind of sugar. It contains carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen. A plant can use glucose for energy. It can also use its atoms to help build other molecules.

Key idea: Photosynthesis moves matter into sugar molecules. Sugar contains atoms that can be reused to build other molecules.
Step 2 of 11

2. Atoms Are Reused to Build New Molecules

All matter is made of atoms. Atoms join in different combinations to form molecules. A molecule is two or more atoms joined by chemical bonds. Glucose is one example.

Living things do not create atoms from nothing when they grow. They take in matter and rearrange its atoms. Atoms from food, water, and air can become part of new molecules. A carbon atom that was once in sugar may later be part of a protein, a lipid, or another molecule.

Not every atom in one sugar molecule must end up in the same product. Atoms can be separated and combined in many ways. Organisms also obtain needed elements from other sources. Plants take up nitrogen compounds from soil. Animals obtain nitrogen by eating food.

All matter is made of atoms. Atoms join to make molecules. Glucose is a molecule.

Living things do not make new atoms from nothing. They take in matter. They rearrange atoms to build new molecules. An atom from sugar may later be part of a protein or another molecule.

Atoms from one sugar do not all have to go into one product. Organisms get other elements from other sources. Plants take in nitrogen compounds through roots. Animals get nitrogen from food.

Remember: Sugar supplies some atoms and carbon-based structures. Other elements must come from the environment or food.
Step 3 of 11

3. Amino Acids Build Proteins

Amino acids are small molecules that cells use as building blocks for proteins. Amino acids contain carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, and nitrogen. Some amino acids also contain sulfur. The carbon-based parts can be built using atoms from sugars. Nitrogen and other needed elements must also be available.

Cells join amino acids in a particular order to make proteins. A protein is a large molecule made of one or more chains of amino acids. The order and shape of the chain affect what the protein can do.

Proteins have many jobs. Some speed up chemical processes. These proteins are called enzymes. Other proteins help build structures, move materials, send signals, or defend the body. Muscle proteins help muscles contract. Enzymes help cells carry out reactions needed for life.

Amino acids are small building blocks. Cells use them to make proteins. Amino acids contain carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, and nitrogen. Some also contain sulfur.

Atoms from sugars can help build the carbon-based parts of amino acids. Cells also need nitrogen and other elements. Cells join amino acids in a chain. The chain forms a protein.

Proteins do many jobs. Some help reactions happen faster. These are enzymes. Other proteins build structures, move materials, send signals, or help defend the body.

Key idea: Organisms need carbon-based material and other elements to make amino acids and proteins.
Step 4 of 11

4. Other Building Blocks Make DNA and RNA

Living things need more than proteins. They also need nucleic acids. DNA and RNA are nucleic acids. They help store and use genetic information.

The building blocks of DNA and RNA are called nucleotides. Each nucleotide contains a sugar, a phosphate group, and a nitrogen-containing base. The sugar in a nucleotide is not glucose, but both contain carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen. Nucleotides also contain nitrogen and phosphorus.

Cells use atoms from many sources to build nucleotides. Carbon-based parts can be made using material from sugars. Nitrogen and phosphorus must also be supplied. Nucleotides join in long chains to form DNA or RNA. DNA stores instructions. RNA helps cells use those instructions to make proteins.

Organisms also build other carbon-based molecules. Lipids help form cell membranes and store energy. Some carbohydrates provide energy or structural support. Each type of molecule has a different structure and job.

Living things need more than proteins. DNA and RNA are nucleic acids. They help store and use genetic information.

DNA and RNA are made from small units called nucleotides. A nucleotide has a sugar, a phosphate group, and a nitrogen-containing base. It contains carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, and phosphorus.

Cells use atoms from different sources to make nucleotides. Nucleotides join to form DNA or RNA. DNA stores instructions. RNA helps cells use the instructions to make proteins.

Cells also make other molecules. Lipids help make cell membranes and store energy. Some carbohydrates provide energy or support.

Compare: Amino acids build proteins. Nucleotides build DNA and RNA. Different building blocks make different large molecules.
Step 5 of 11

5. Matter and Energy in Living Systems

Matter and energy move through living systems, but they are not the same thing. Matter is the material that makes up organisms. It includes atoms and molecules. Energy makes change and activity possible.

Photosynthesis stores some light energy in the chemical bonds of sugars. Cells can release usable energy from sugars through cellular respiration. Cells use energy to grow, repair parts, move materials, and build molecules. Building proteins and DNA requires both matter and energy.

Think about a growing plant. It takes in carbon dioxide, water, and minerals. It uses light energy to make sugars. It uses atoms from these materials to make new cells and molecules. Some matter becomes part of the plant. Some matter leaves the plant in other forms. The atoms are rearranged, not destroyed.

Matter is the material that makes up living things. Atoms and molecules are matter. Energy helps living things do work.

Photosynthesis stores light energy in sugar. Cells can release usable energy from sugar through cellular respiration. Cells use energy to grow, repair, move materials, and build molecules.

A growing plant takes in carbon dioxide, water, and minerals. It uses light to make sugar. It uses atoms from these materials to make new cells. Atoms are rearranged. They are not destroyed.

Do not confuse them: Atoms are matter. Energy is not an atom or a building block. Energy powers processes that rearrange matter.
Step 6 of 11

6. Use Evidence to Explain the Process

Scientists use models to show how parts of a system relate. A model may use boxes, arrows, symbols, or pictures. An arrow may show that matter or energy moves from one part to another. Read the labels to see what the arrow means.

Evidence can come from a model, simulation, or data. Evidence is information that supports or challenges an explanation. A strong explanation makes a claim, gives relevant evidence, and explains how the evidence supports the claim.

For example, a model may show carbon dioxide entering a plant and carbon becoming part of sugar. Another model may show atoms from sugar and nitrogen being used to make amino acids. Together, these models support the idea that photosynthesis provides some of the matter used to build molecules in living things.

Scientists revise explanations when new evidence shows that an earlier explanation was incomplete or incorrect. Revising does not mean guessing. It means using evidence to make the explanation more accurate.

Scientists use models to show how a system works. Models use pictures, boxes, symbols, and arrows. Read the labels. An arrow can show where matter or energy moves.

Evidence is information that helps support or challenge an explanation. Evidence can come from a model, a simulation, or data.

A good explanation has a claim, evidence, and reasoning. The claim answers the question. The evidence supports the claim. The reasoning explains why the evidence supports the claim.

Scientists change an explanation when new evidence shows that it needs improvement. They use evidence, not guesses.

Build an explanation:
Claim: Sugar made during photosynthesis can help supply atoms for other molecules.
Evidence: Sugar contains carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen. Models show these atoms being used with other elements to form building blocks such as amino acids.
Reasoning: Cells rearrange atoms from available materials to make molecules needed for life.

Question 6 · Use evidence and revise

A first model shows sugar supplying carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen for building molecules. New evidence shows that nitrogen is also part of amino acids. What is the best revision?

Practice answer key — check after trying
  1. B. Less light means less photosynthesis and usually less new sugar. Sugar is one source of carbon-based material used to build other molecules.
  2. C. Amino acids contain carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, and nitrogen. Cells can use carbon-based material from sugars along with nitrogen from another source.
  3. A. The bright-light group had the most sugar and the most new protein. This pattern supports a relationship in these data, but it does not show that sugar is the only requirement.
  4. D. Glucose contains carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen, but amino acids also need nitrogen. Some amino acids contain sulfur. Plants obtain these elements from their surroundings.
  5. B. Matter is conserved in ordinary chemical processes. Atoms are rearranged and joined in new combinations; they are not created or destroyed.
  6. C. The revised model should include nitrogen as another input. Sugar still supplies carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen that can contribute to building molecules.

Explain, Use Evidence, and Revise

Your question: How can sugar made by photosynthesis help a plant build proteins and other large carbon-based molecules?

Step 1: Write your first explanation

Use the lesson models and the practice data. Make a claim, use at least two specific pieces of evidence, and explain how the evidence supports your claim. Include how matter and energy have different roles. You may write a paragraph or clearly labeled bullet points.

Sentence starters: Photosynthesis helps a plant build molecules because ___. The model in section ___ shows ___. The data show ___. This evidence supports my claim because ___.

Step 2: Open new evidence after writing your first explanation

New evidence: In an illustrative investigation, similar plants received the same light, water, and carbon dioxide. One group received enough nitrogen compounds. The other received very little nitrogen. Both groups made sugar, but the group with very little nitrogen made less new protein.

Amino acids contain nitrogen as well as carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen. Some also contain sulfur. Nucleotides contain nitrogen and phosphorus as well as carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen.

Think: Does your first explanation suggest that sugar alone is enough? What input would you add to a model? Where would a plant obtain it? Keep ideas supported by evidence and improve incomplete ones.

Step 3: Revise using the new evidence

Revise your explanation to account for the nitrogen evidence. Explain what sugars contribute, which other elements are needed, where plants get those elements, and how atoms are conserved. Describe how the resulting molecules support at least one life process, such as growth or repair.

Step 4: Check your explanation

  • I explain how photosynthesis supplies sugars containing carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen.
  • I use two specific pieces of evidence from the models or data and explain what they show.
  • I explain that other elements, including nitrogen, must come from other sources.
  • I describe atoms being rearranged rather than created, and distinguish matter from energy.
  • I connect building molecules to a life process and explain my revision using the new evidence.
